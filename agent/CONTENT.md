## Research

Current research focus:
Legionella research at Virginia Tech.

Research statement:
My research explores how microbial communities interact with the built water environment and how those interactions influence the persistence, transport, and control of opportunistic pathogens. I am particularly interested in understanding microbial ecology not as an isolated biological process, but as something shaped by the physical, chemical, and engineered environments through which microorganisms move and grow.

My approach brings together multiple perspectives and methods to understand these relationships. I combine environmental engineering and water-quality concepts with microbial ecology, molecular biology, and quantitative approaches to examine how microorganisms interact with one another and with water infrastructure. My work incorporates tools such as flow cytometry, direct digital PCR, fluorescence in situ hybridization, molecular detection, genomics, and metagenomics alongside measurements of environmental conditions and engineering characteristics.

A central focus of my current research is the ecology of Legionella pneumophila and other opportunistic microorganisms in potable water distribution and premise plumbing systems. I am interested in how microbial communities, biofilms, sediments, and engineered surfaces influence pathogen persistence and transport, and in how interactions between microorganisms may create opportunities for new approaches to pathogen control.

More broadly, I am interested in bringing together approaches that are often studied separately: microbial community ecology, pathogen biology, water treatment, and infrastructure engineering. By connecting these perspectives, my research seeks to develop a more complete understanding of why opportunistic pathogens persist in engineered water systems and how that knowledge can be translated into practical strategies for protecting public health.

An emerging direction of this work examines host–symbiont relationships as a potential component of microbial management in premise plumbing. This work considers whether naturally occurring biological interactions can be understood and potentially applied as part of new strategies for managing microbial communities in engineered water environments.
